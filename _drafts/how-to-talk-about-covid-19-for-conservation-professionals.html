It goes without saying that we're all currently living though a time of uncertainty and personal crisis, so - while I'm sure you're growing very used to seeing these words at the top of every email - I hope you and your communities are going okay.

Every day our communication and use of language matters, but during a crisis like this the impact of communication becomes particularly clear.

But we can all have a positive influence on the language and social discourse around this crisis by being thoughtful about our own conversations and encouraging thoughtful and strategic communication approaches in our own workplace and community groups.

Below are my main recommendations for how to talk about COVID-19, drawing from my own strategic communications experience and some of the fantastic advice and resources published recently online (see below for links).

Be generous and understanding

First off, don't forget that everyone is - at the very least -  stressed and anxious right now. Many people have lost their jobs, and everyone is going through changes in their everyday lifestyle and worklife. So, be considerate and aware of what everyone is going through, consider when others may be more seriously impacted than yourself, and make sure this is reflected in the tone and approach of your communications.

Be accurate

Help to prevent the spread of misinformation but checking your sources before you pass on information. Don't reshare infographics or lists of recommendations without checking where they come from first. Listen to authorities like the World Health Organisation and government departments when it comes to sharing recommendations. The stakes for miscommunication of health information and noncompliance with recommendations are higher in a crisis situation.

Be clear on the purpose of your communication

Part of being generous and understanding is being clear on the purpose of your communications and considering carefully what exactly you are asking of your listeners or readers. Having a clear purpose is a key basis for any kind of strategic communication, but it is particularly important during a crisis such as COVID-19, when emotions are high.

Most communications around COVID-19 will be aiming to inform or educate the public or encourage compliance with local government recommendations, or (more often) both! There are many different communication approaches and strategies that can be applied for both these aims but for the purposes of this blog post I will discuss two broad kinds of communication: science communication and strategic communication.

Science communication (e.g. increasing public understanding of how COVID-19 spreads, explaining prediction models, debunking myths etc.)

Examples: ABC's Coronacast, RMIT's Fact Check

Science communication has a crucial role to play in COVID-19 communications, particularly around clear communication around how COVID-19 spreads and how individuals can protect themselves and their communities. Clear communication of uncertainty - a massive communication challenge - is also very important for effectively communicating the uncertainty of particularly predictions or models around the spread of the virus or uncertainty around the predicted effectiveness of different interventions.

Science communication traditionally uses the knowledge-deficit model: the idea that people will change how they think and behave if they understand the science. In a public health crisis situation like the COVID-19 crisis, this approach can be relatively effective as members of the public tend to be paying more attention to expert advice and may therefore be more likely to take it on board.

However, since humans are not always entirely rational beings, we cannot rely entirely on traditional science communication approaches to actually trigger changes in human behaviour and compliance with recommendations.

Strategic communication (e.g. increasing public compliance with physical distancing recommendations, fostering a culture of community and optimism)

Examples: Dan Andrews' social media profiles, ACF's covid update

When we are trying to encourage people to act a certain way (e.g. physical distancing) with our communications a strategic communications approach is appropriate. Strategic communications is a umbrella term than encompasses many kinds of purposeful communications approaches, including crisis and risk communication and public engagement approaches.

In all kinds of strategic communication being clear about your intent is vital, but with crisis or risk communication the stakes are usually higher, as is the case with COVID-19. Consider what you want your listeners or readers to do as a results of your communication. Does the tone, structure and focus of your communication do that effectively? For example, it may be sometimes tempting to use social media to vent about an issue you are passionate about, but if you are ultimately wanting to encourage people to act in a certain way, that may not be the most suitable approach, depending on the audience. Consider carefully your purpose, your audience and your approach before you post.

Be careful with your language

Small changes in the language we use can have substantial impacts on understanding and perceptions around an issue. Below are some consistent recommendations around language around COVID-19. Check out the further reading or check out Dr Suzanne Wertheim's Twitter thread below for more detail and research around these tips.

  • Describe numbers of detected cases as "known cases" instead of "cases"
  • Make human agency explicit by describing how "we spread the virus by going outside" rather than "the virus is spreading itself"
  • Use social norming (i.e. make it about me, us, we, our community) to make the issue personally and socially relevant, and communicate how "most Australians are doing the right thing by staying home" rather than saying "people aren't staying home!" (us humans like doing what other poeple are doing!)
  • Describe staying at home as "physical distancing" rather than "social distancing" to convey how we still want people to stay connected and social but taking care of each other by staying away
  • Emphasise kindness, community and cooperation rather than fear, othering or panic

Can we still talk about biodiversity and climate change?

It's becoming increasingly clear that coronavirus is already having a dramatic impact on our everyday life, and many nations and states have declared states of emergency. So in the short term, we need to carefully consider the relevance and appropriateness of our conservation messaging and be especially sensitive to the personal and social context of the individuals we are trying to engage.

However, it is worth being aware of situations where conservation work and messaging continues to be needed. For example, with climate talks being postponed and the public focused on COVID-19 we run the risk of important actions being delayed or ignored.

Where do we want this story to go?

Looking longer term, it now seems undeniable that coronavirus is changing the world, and the social context in which we continue to research and work will be different even if we go eventually return 'back to normal'.

As conservation scientists we don't want to see COVID-19 create a new social context where actions on issues like biodiversity conservation or climate change are even more difficult. As communicators then, we need to start to think about where we want the story to go. Perhaps economic reform as a result of COVID-19 could also make progress towards a more sustainable future? Perhaps rather than bouncing back, we can bounce forward after coronavirus?

We can start to lay the foundation for these conversations by avoid comparing crises (e.g. by lamenting that biodiversity conservation hasn’t had the same response as COVID-19) and focus instead on needing kindness and cooperation, and scientific expertise. Some responses to COVID-19 convey border control and security message frames that don’t set a healthy narrative context for action for biodiversity conservation or climate change.

In doing this we foster a narrative of cooperation amidst a crisis, where we listen to the advice of experts, and are able to find solutions to complex and global problems, setting the stage for progress towards a more sustainable future.
