I've always loved writing, and I've always considered that writing is something that I do.

Yet, when I really think about it, my habits in the non-academic writing sector are definitely subpar. (In the academic sector of course, my habits are rock solid...).

I've always tended to be the when-the-mood-strikes-me kind of writer, which leads to a rollercoaster of productivity and little significant output. It's only this year I've started setting aside an hour at the end of each weekday to write, and those hours haven't necessarily been very productive. When I'm not falling prey to the usual suspects (social media, emails), I'm spending time on other tasks which vary from necessary research for science communication posts to feeling guilty about not working on my PhD.

Pretty much all of these procrastinating tasks boil down to some element of imposter syndrome. We're all familiar with both the phrase and the feeling but I'm a scientist by training so here's a quoted definition from Mirriam-Webster:

"Originally called impostor phenomenon, impostor syndrome, as it's now usually called, is commonly understood as a false and sometimes crippling belief that one's successes are the product of luck or fraud rather than skill."

Now, of course, in assuming what I'm experiencing is imposter syndrome, I'm also assuming my reasons for feeling like an imposter is unwarranted but let's not open that can of worms (instead we'll leave it...dangling here, without even a full stop or right parentheses for closure

All of these procrastinating tasks boil down to imposter syndrome because they are almost entirely driven by the fear of that belief. Because if I get a fact wrong, someone will realise I'm an idiot. If I miss a typo, someone will think I'm careless.

Because, why the hell am I writing this post anyway? Who am I to have a take on this? This isn't really my area. I haven't written something decent in ages. Someone else could do this way better. I'm just adding to the already torrential downpour of blog posts on the internet. What's the point?

At least that's what the Imposter Syndrome Chicken in my head keeps saying to me.

So in the interest of kicking the poultry out (and pumping out a blog post, not going to lie...), I present how I procrastinate when #IShouldBeWriting and why we all should just chill out, tone down the perfectionism, and just start writing.

1.  Spending an excessive amount of time on research and incessant fact-checking

This has been one of my major issues with writing science communication pieces.

First, how much research is necessary? Is this an opinion piece or something I know enough about to just write myself? Is it a read-one-paper-and-write-about-it piece? An I-need-to-directly-interview-three-people piece?

It's important to answer that question, but it's more important to answer that question sensibly, while considering your desired post type and frequency and how best to meet those goals.

Second, how important is it to fact check every single line? Obviously we don't want to include something that isn't backed by evidence, but the level of rigour our science training has instilled can sometimes feels like overkill when writing a short blog post. Be open about what is uncertain, and research carefully what is central to the post and any specific statistics you quote, but don't get caught up in the detail. Just be open about what is evidence-based, what is uncertain, and what is your opinion.

2. Reading blog posts encouraging me NOT to write blog posts

I'm not going to link to any of these incredibly discouraging posts because I don't want to pass on the sentiments. Basically just re-read what the Imposter Syndrome Chicken said above and imagine those arguments coming from actual real (probably) people on the internet.

Basically, you get to decide how you want to spend your time, and if you want to write science communication posts, you should write science communication posts. If you want to write science communication posts for an audience you have to put some thought into how you're going to target that audience, but still no one can tell you such work is pointless or you are the wrong person to be doing it. (They are allowed to give you constructive feedback, but that's a good thing!)

Consider carefully how personal you really want to go in blog posts (particularly professional ones), but don't feel like you have to keep yourself entirely seperate from your work (if you don't want to!). Any potential employer who dismisses your application because your professional blog once mentioned your cat is probably not an employer you want to have.

3. Editing and proofreading as if it's my final thesis draft just before submission

You're not gunning for a New York Times Bestseller.

Also, if you notice massive issues later you have the ability to edit (Wordpress isn't Twitter). Give it the once or twice over (no more!) and just press that damn 'Publish...' button.

5. Feeling guilty about not working on my PhD

Because your PhD is your life now, right? Wrong - your PhD is your worklife now, and while that may be a big part of your life, and a PhD worklife is quite possibly one of the biggest asks of a worklife, it is still not your life!

For your own sake run, hike, swim, draw, climb, read, watch Netflix, and write for something other than a paper or thesis! And don't you dare feel guilty about it.
