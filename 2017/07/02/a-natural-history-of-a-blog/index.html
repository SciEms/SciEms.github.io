A Natural History of a Blog

I once read a novel by Sir David Attenborough, one he wrote while filming his first TV series, Zoo Quest.

I have always loved Attenborough’s documentaries and his writing was equally compelling. I was right there with him as he slept in a leaking shack during a South American downpour. I was wading through bogs and fighting off chiggers. I was releasing rare hummingbirds with my own two hands. It was invigorating.

These imaginary (as well as real) experiences are part of what drew me to studying science. I wanted to learn more. I wanted to understand. I wanted to trek through deserts, forests, mountains, and plains, chasing animals along the way. This aspiration led me to ecology, scientific research, writing reports, and coding. I love it to pieces.

But before I was a scientist, I was a reader, and I will always be a reader, a word which here means "a person prone to going on adventures whilst sitting on trams or drinking tea in bed, whenever doing so is preferable to actual adventures."

And as a reader with a penchant for science, old natural history novels have always appealed to me. Their enchanting mix of science and travel writing draws you in, and you blissfully soak up knowledge as if you were on holiday someplace far far away.

There is a clear strain of optimism in such work that is hard to find within science or environmental pieces today (and not without fair reason). In contrast, these old texts are filled with the joy of discovery, the toxicity of curiosity, the jump-up-and-down-in-your-chair-can't-control-yourself love of an idea, a world, an environment, a process, anything!

The writings of early explorers, for example, were not strictly scientific, but told the story of an age of discovery within natural history; both ground-breaking and enchanting to those waiting back home. These people were explorers and scientists, but they were also storytellers.

I'm similarly inspired by those “Renaissance men” who engaged with multiple disciplines; the polymaths, including the likes of Leonardo Da Vinci, Benjamin Franklin, and Winston Churchill. These people were exceptional scientists, politicians, inventors, artists, and writers. Yet in the end, it’s their interest in and value of these varying areas that is inspiring to me, rather than their level of skill.

The arts and sciences are often drawn as opposites, and I believe this is not only a saddening, but truly misguided trend. Ultimately, the ‘Arts’ and the ‘Sciences’ are trying to do the same thing: further our knowledge and understanding. The methods often differ, because the subjects differ, but the underlying motivation is the same: to learn, and to understand.

And I truly see having a simple appreciation for different areas of knowledge as being most important; certainly, more important than being ‘smart’ or ‘talented’. As well as encompassing a simple love of learning, there is something practically valuable in interdisciplinary work. Only by considering many different perspectives can we hope to solve the issues facing our people and world today.

And in my experience, one of the easiest ways to gain a new perspective is to read a book, or a journal article, or an opinion piece. (Another is to actually talk to a human person, but we all know those things are scary). In short, stories are important. Both fictional and non-fictional.

Scientific research in and of itself is vital, but if it cannot be communicated effectively then those endless hours of work may not have the impact they ought to. Whether this impact is on policy, management, or public opinion, the loss is a mighty shame.

Which brings me to the introduction of ‘Lizard Tales’: a blog which will drag you out of your house and into lab rooms, deserts, and dusty 4WDs.

Together we will explore the mysterious realm of modern scientific research, and follow the stories and characters whose very existence proves that the Age of Discovery is never over.
